# Testing the lag hinted at in the assignment notes directly, rather than
# assuming it: at which time delay does each relationship actually peak?
lag_range <- seq(0, 24, by = 3)

inflation_lag_corr <- map_dbl(lag_range, function(k) {
  cor(fed_data$fed_funds_rate, dplyr::lag(fed_data$inflation_yoy, k), use = "complete.obs")
})

fedfunds_lag_corr <- map_dbl(lag_range, function(k) {
  cor(fed_data$unemployment_rate, dplyr::lag(fed_data$fed_funds_rate, k), use = "complete.obs")
})

lag_data <- tibble(
  lag_months = rep(lag_range, 2),
  correlation = c(inflation_lag_corr, fedfunds_lag_corr),
  relationship = rep(c("Fed Funds Rate vs. Past Inflation", "Unemployment vs. Past Fed Funds Rate"),
                      each = length(lag_range))
)

ggplot(lag_data, aes(x = lag_months, y = correlation)) +

Story Notes

We use CPI (CPIAUCSL) rather than core PCE for inflation, per the assignment’s guidance that inflation is defined as the rate of change of CPI; this is a broader, more volatile measure than the core PCE the Fed itself targets, which is part of why our inflation readings run hotter than the Fed’s own 2% framing at times. Era boundaries are drawn directly from the assignment’s own dates: Pre-FAIT ends and FAIT begins in August 2020, and FAIT ends in August 2025.

For the era-comparison figure, we exclude the acute COVID shock months (March-July 2020) from each era’s average. Those months reflect a pandemic lockdown, not a monetary-policy choice, and including them would make any era’s average reflect an exogenous shock rather than the policy actually being evaluated.

For the lag figure, we tested a range of delays rather than assuming one, since the assignment notes that policy and its effects don’t happen instantly. The Fed Funds Rate correlates most strongly with inflation from 12-18 months earlier, consistent with a real, measurable delay in how the Fed responds to price data. Unemployment, however, correlates most strongly with the Fed Funds Rate in the same month, not a later one. We read this as evidence the relationship runs in both directions at once, not as a simple one-way lag: rate hikes do eventually weigh on jobs, but the Fed also cuts rates reactively as unemployment rises, which pulls the strongest correlation back toward the present rather than the past.
